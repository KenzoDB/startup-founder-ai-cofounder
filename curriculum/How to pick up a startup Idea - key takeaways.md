Don't try to find the perfect idea in the abstract — you can only figure out the right idea by making contact with reality and getting customer feedback.
Stop working on multiple ideas simultaneously; it produces bad signal. Commit to one, 'burn the other boats,' and go deep.
Going deep means changing your identity around the idea: rename the company, rewrite emails and website, change your internal narrative — become unrecognizable.
The depth test: could you actually run your customer's business? Could you teach a class on the problem? You should be one of the most informed people in the world on the subject.
You don't need a decade of domain experience; if you're curious, go extremely deep and talk to customers, and you can develop extraordinary knowledge quickly (e.g., Blake Scholl went from ad tech to supersonic aviation at Boom).
Run a tight loop: deep customer understanding → product delivery → deeper understanding → better product. Don't wait to talk to hundreds of customers before writing code.
In the AI era, look for ideas at the edge of what models can do today — barely working on frontier models but clearly improving as models improve. The bottleneck itself might become the company.
Verticalize: sell outcomes (be the insurer, be the bank), not just software for incumbents. As software costs go to zero, value accrues to trust, licenses, regulatory permission, and outcome ownership.
Pursue the most ambitious version of your idea — the cost of ambitious and modest startups is roughly the same, but ambitious ones attract better talent, have stronger moats, and rewrite sectors.
Even if the idea fails after going deep, you gain unambiguous customer data, real conviction for a pivot, and often discover the better idea underneath — the deeper structural problem.
The worst failure mode isn't being wrong — it's not making a decision and spinning your wheels across multiple ideas without going deep enough on any.
